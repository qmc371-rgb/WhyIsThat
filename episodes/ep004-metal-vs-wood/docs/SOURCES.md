## Sources
1. **American Physical Society, "Getting Warmer / Getting Colder"** (learning resource). 🔁
   https://www.aps.org/learning-resources/getting-warmer-colder
   Metal and wood at room temperature: metal feels colder because its higher thermal conductivity
   draws heat from your fingers faster; ice melts more quickly on a high-conductivity material.
2. **American Chemical Society, Middle School Chemistry, Chapter 2 Lesson 1.** 🔁 **not confirmed**:
   search didn't surface the page text. Cited in the draft at L19; L17–L19 are also covered by 1 and 3.
   https://www.acs.org/middleschoolchemistry/lessonplans/chapter2/lesson1.html
3. **University of Illinois Physics Van, "Why do Metals Feel Cold or Hot to the Touch?"** 🔁
   https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/44029
   Metals feel colder or hotter than other materials at the same temperature because they conduct heat well.
4. **HyperPhysics (Georgia State University), "Steel and Wood at Zero Celsius."** 🔁
   https://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/thermo/woodmet.html
   At 0 °C steel feels colder than wood; at 100 °C steel feels hotter: it conducts energy faster
   either way. (The video uses no temperatures; the reverse case is a diagram only.)

## Deliberate limits (from the brief)
- Arrows always point from warmer to cooler; no "cold particles" entering a hand.
- On-screen note: touch also depends on heat capacity, density, contact, coatings and geometry;
  conductivity is the introductory focus.
- Substantial blocks for the ice, never foil. Melting is qualitative: no timings, footage or temperatures.
- The reverse case is a diagram with no hand on a dangerously hot surface.
- No infrared-thermometer "proof" (emissivity of shiny metal distorts it).
- Every diagram is labelled "Simplified illustration."
