You record a quick message.

Nothing special. Just a few sentences.

Then you press play.

And immediately think…

“Wait. That’s my voice?”

It sounds thinner. Maybe higher. Somehow less like you than the voice you’ve been hearing your entire life.

Your first suspect is the phone.

But there’s something else involved.

When you speak, you get a version of your voice that nobody standing beside you hears in quite the same way.

Why is that?

Start with an ordinary conversation.

Your voice creates vibrations in the air.

Those vibrations travel from your mouth to the people around you—and to your own ears.

That route is called air conduction.

But while you’re speaking, another route is working at the same time.

Vibrations also travel through the bones and tissues of your head to your inner ear.

That’s part of what’s called bone conduction.

So the voice you hear while talking is a combination.

Sound arriving through the air.

And vibrations arriving through your own head.

Imagine two paths on a map.

One leaves your mouth, travels through the room, and reaches your ears.

The other travels internally.

Both contribute to the experience you recognize as your voice.

Now bring in the phone.

An ordinary microphone records the sound reaching it through the air.

It doesn’t capture and recreate the vibrations traveling through your head while you speak.

When you play the recording through an ordinary speaker, you hear it through a different listening situation.

You’ve gone from being the person producing the sound to being a listener hearing it from a device.

The voice is yours.

The route has changed.

And that route affects the sound.

Your head doesn’t transmit every part of your voice equally.

Bone and tissue change the balance of frequencies reaching your inner ear.

That helps explain why a recording can seem thinner or less full than the voice you expect.

But this isn’t a universal bass setting we can copy exactly with one audio filter.

Researchers studying self-voice perception also point to the physical sensations accompanying bone conduction.

Speaking involves more than passively listening to a sound.

You’re producing it and experiencing its vibrations at the same time.

Think about what that means.

The voice most familiar to you is tied to the act of speaking.

A recording removes that act.

You hear the words without making them.

You hear the timing without controlling it.

And you hear your voice arriving from somewhere else.

That’s a surprisingly different experience for something you’ve been doing all your life.

This raises the question everyone asks next.

“Is the recording what I really sound like to other people?”

It captures the airborne sound that other listeners receive, rather than your private combination of airborne sound and internal vibrations.

In that sense, it gives you a different perspective on your voice.

But a phone recording isn’t a perfect copy of another person’s experience.

The microphone, its position, the room, any audio processing, and the playback speaker can all affect the result.

A recording is a useful perspective.

It isn’t a final verdict on your voice.

And sounding unfamiliar doesn’t automatically mean sounding bad.

The people you talk to aren’t expecting the internally transmitted version you hear.

They know you from the voice that reaches them.

You’re the one encountering a different listening perspective.

The fascinating part is that hearing doesn’t depend only on the sound being produced.

It also depends on how vibrations reach the inner ear.

Air is one route.

Bone is another.

Your own voice happens to give you an everyday experience of both.

So next time you press play and think, “That can’t be me”…

Remember the two paths.

While speaking, you hear sound traveling through the room and vibrations traveling through your head.

An ordinary recording doesn’t recreate that same combination.

You haven’t suddenly acquired a strange new voice.

You’re hearing a familiar voice from a less familiar position.

Everyday things. Surprising explanations.
