Arby’s.

R. B.

Roast beef.

That sounds like a mystery solved.

Except those letters stand for something else.

And once you learn the stories behind these four restaurant names, you might never read their signs quite the same way again.

Let’s start with the sandwich.

Picture a roast-beef sandwich beside two enormous letters.

R and B.

Say them together.

“Arby.”

It’s such a convincing explanation that you barely have to think about it.

But according to Arby’s parent company, the name refers to the Raffel Brothers: Leroy and Forrest.

They founded the restaurant in Boardman, Ohio, in 1964.

Raffel Brothers.

R. B.

Arby’s.

The initials belong to the founders. The roast beef makes the coincidence especially easy to believe.

That’s what makes this a good naming mystery.

The answer seems to be sitting right there on the menu.

But the real explanation is standing behind the counter.

Two brothers whose surname became something millions of customers would say when ordering lunch.

Now let’s move from two brothers to one daughter.

Who was Wendy?

If your answer is “the founder’s daughter,” you’re on the right track.

But there’s another detail.

Her name was Melinda.

Dave Thomas considered his children’s names before choosing Melinda’s nickname for the restaurant.

Wendy’s official history explains that her siblings had trouble pronouncing her name. “Wenda” eventually became “Wendy.”

A family nickname became a restaurant name.

Imagine that transformation.

At home, it’s a name your brothers and sisters call you.

Outside, it becomes a sign above a restaurant.

Then customers start saying it without knowing anything about the family behind it.

The name sounds like a person because it came from one.

But the story sits one layer beneath the obvious answer.

Wendy was real.

Wendy was also a nickname.

Now for a name with something you can actually picture.

You hear “Taco Bell,” and the words seem perfectly matched to the logo.

A taco restaurant.

A bell.

But the Bell is also a person.

Glen Bell founded the chain, opening its first restaurant in Downey, California, in 1962.

His surname is right there in the brand.

Once you know that, the name reads differently.

It isn’t simply a food followed by a familiar object.

It’s the food followed by the founder.

The bell symbol makes the name easy to visualize.

But the family name gives it another meaning.

We’ve now found brothers, a daughter’s nickname, and a founder hiding in plain sight.

Our last stop takes us somewhere quite different.

A coffee shop name that leads to a map—and a novel.

Before we reveal this one, take a guess.

Was Starbucks named after a founder?

A type of coffee?

Or a character in a book?

The book is part of the answer.

Starbucks’ own account describes a naming process that included the discovery of “Starbo” on an old map.

That brought the team back to Starbuck—the first mate aboard the Pequod in Herman Melville’s Moby-Dick.

The name we associate with coffee has a literary and seafaring connection.

It wasn’t simply a matter of opening a book and pointing at a page.

The sound of a place name helped lead them toward a character’s name.

Picture the journey on screen.

An old map.

The word “Starbo.”

A book opening.

“Starbuck.”

Then the familiar coffee-shop name.

A surprising route to something we now read without a second thought.

Four familiar names.

Two brothers.

A founder’s surname.

And a trail from an old map to a literary sailor.

You don’t need to know any of those stories to order lunch.

But knowing them changes what you notice.

Next time you pass one of those signs, you’ll see more than a restaurant name.

You’ll see a little piece of how it began.
